## Track 0: Three Reports

Terrence Whitfield fixed things for a living. For three years he had worked maintenance for a school district in Phoenix. He changed light ballasts. He unclogged drinking fountains. He kept a small notebook in his shirt pocket and wrote down every part he used.

One week in August, his own car needed fixing, and it was the kind of fixing he could not do. The mechanic said the transmission was gone. The mechanic said the repair would cost more than the car was worth.

So after his shift, Terrence drove the car one last careful time to Valley Credit Union. He had paid off his last car loan there. The statement in his glovebox said the balance was zero dollars. He was proud of that statement.

The loan officer was a woman named Ruth Okafor. She wore reading glasses on a chain, and she had a cup of coffee she never drank.

@Terrence"I'm looking at a used car," Terrence said. @Terrence"I want to know what kind of rate I'd get."

Ruth folded her hands.

@Ruth"That depends on what's in your credit reports," Ruth said. @Ruth"Have you looked at them lately?"

@Terrence"I figured there was one report," Terrence said. @Terrence"And I figured it was bad."

Ruth shook her head.

@Ruth"There are three," Ruth said. @Ruth"Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion. Three different companies. Each one keeps its own file on you, and they don't always agree. You can get all three for free, every week, at Annual Credit Report dot com. Pull all three before you let anybody run your credit for a loan."

@Terrence"Every week," Terrence said. @Terrence"For free."

@Ruth"For free," Ruth said. @Ruth"A lender is going to read those reports. You should read them first."

The next afternoon, after his shift, Terrence took the bus to the Burton Barr Central Library. He signed in at a computer and pulled all three reports. He printed them. Six pages. Each one had the same date at the top: August 12, 2026. He folded them into thirds and carried them home in his jacket.

He cleared the kitchen table. He put the Experian report on the left, the Equifax report in the middle, and the TransUnion report on the right. Then he sat down with a pencil, the same way he sat down with a broken boiler.

The first thing he read was his own name. The Experian report said: Terrence A Whitfield. Also reported as T A Whitfield. That was fine.

Then he read the addresses. The Experian report listed two. The first was 1418 West Cambridge Avenue, where he lived now. The second was 2205 East Roosevelt Street, Apartment 4, where he had lived from 2017 to 2022. Both were right.

He picked up the Equifax report. It listed the same two addresses. Then it listed a third. The Equifax report said: an address in Fresno, California.

Terrence had never lived in Fresno. He had never been to Fresno. He put a small question mark next to it.

Then he turned to the part he had been dreading. The Experian report put it right at the top, under a heading that said Potentially Negative Items.

The report said: Desert Sun Auto Finance. Auto loan. Opened June 2019. Original amount, fourteen thousand nine hundred dollars. Charged off. Balance, eight thousand four hundred twelve dollars. Closed November 2022.

Terrence knew that line. He did not need a pencil for that one.

In 2022, Rio Vista Warehousing had cut his whole shift. Two weeks later, he spent two nights in the hospital. He came home to the Roosevelt Street apartment with the bracelet still on his wrist and a stack of bills on the floor. By November, the car was gone. A tow truck took it from the curb while he watched from the window.

That line was true. He did not like it, but it was true.

He kept reading. There were medical collections, and the three reports did not agree on them.

The Equifax report said: Anchor Recovery. Medical collection. One thousand nine hundred dollars.

Terrence got up and went to the hall closet. He kept a plastic file box there. He found a folder labeled Hospital 2022. Inside was a letter from Anchor Recovery. The letter said the account was paid in full. He had paid it fifty dollars at a time.

He set the letter next to the Equifax report. Then he checked Experian. Experian did not list Anchor Recovery at all.

@Terrence"So one of you knows I paid it," Terrence said to the table, @Terrence"and one of you doesn't."

There was another one. The Experian report said: Phoenix General. Eight hundred forty dollars. A few lines down, under a different name, the same report said: Sierra Asset. Eight hundred forty dollars. Same amount. Different name. Terrence wrote both names in his notebook and drew an arrow between them, with another question mark.

And there was a small one. The report said: Meridian. Three hundred ten dollars.

Then he read the accounts in good standing. The Copper State Visa, which he paid off every month, showed a balance of three hundred forty dollars. The Valley Credit Union auto loan showed a balance of zero. The Summit store card was there too. At least, it was there on two of the three.

Near the end of each report was a list of hard inquiries. Those are the times a lender pulled his file because he applied for something. Experian showed two. Equifax showed three. TransUnion showed one.

By the end, all six pages were covered in pencil. Question marks. Arrows. Small check marks next to the lines that were right.

He sat back. Three companies had written down the story of his life with money, and none of the three stories matched. One had a city he had never seen. Two still listed a bill he had already paid. One listed the same bill twice, under two different names.

A lender might pull just one of the three. Terrence did not get to pick which one.

He looked at the three stacks for a long time. Then he turned to a clean page in his notebook. At the top he wrote three words: Experian. Equifax. TransUnion. Under them he drew three columns.

@Terrence"All right," Terrence said. @Terrence"What do you three actually say about me? And which parts are true?"
