## Track 0: Paid in Full

On Monday night, Terrence went back to the library. He brought his notebook, the three reports, and the letter from Anchor Recovery.

Over the weekend, he had sorted his notebook into two lists. The first list was called True. It had the Desert Sun repossession on it, and the Rapid Cash inquiry. Those lines hurt, but they were his.

The second list was called Wrong. He had written it out bureau by bureau.

Experian had two problems. Meridian, $310, was under $500, so it should not be there. Phoenix General and Sierra Asset were the same $840 bill, listed twice.

Equifax had three problems. Anchor Recovery, $1,900, was paid. Meridian, $310, was there too. And there was the address in Fresno, California, where he had never lived.

TransUnion had two problems. Anchor Recovery, $1,900, paid. Meridian, $310.

He went to each bureau's website and found the page for disputes. For every line on his Wrong list, the site asked him to explain. He kept it short. This account is paid in full. This debt is listed twice. I have never lived at this address.

For Anchor Recovery, he took a photo of the paid-in-full letter and uploaded it.

At the end, each site gave him a confirmation number. Each one also showed the same message. The screen said: Most disputes are completed within 30 days.

He copied all three confirmation numbers into his notebook. Then he went home.

At the kitchen table, he opened the file box again. This time he pulled out last month's Copper State Visa statement. He had already paid it. He had written a note on it in pencil: Paid in full, August tenth.

He went down the statement one line at a time.

The statement said: Closing date, July eighteenth. New balance, $340. Minimum payment due, $35. Payment due date, August twelfth. Credit limit, $1,200. Purchase APR, 23.99%.

Near the bottom was a box with a bold heading. The box said: Minimum Payment Warning. If you make only the minimum payment each period, you will pay more in interest and it will take you longer to pay off your balance.

Terrence frowned. He had paid this card to zero. But all three reports, pulled on August twelfth, said he owed $340.

On Tuesday, at lunch, he sat in the boiler room with the statement on his knee. He called Valley Credit Union and asked for Ruth Okafor, the loan officer who had helped him the week before.

Ruth picked up on the second ring. @Ruth"This is Ruth."

@Terrence"It's Terrence Whitfield. I filed the disputes last night. All three bureaus."

@Ruth"Good. Then you wait about thirty days. Don't apply for that car loan until you see what comes back."

@Terrence"I can wait. But I've got a card statement in my hand, and it doesn't make sense to me."

@Ruth"Start at the top."

Terrence ran his thumb down to the rate. @Terrence"It says the APR is 23.99%. I've had this card eight years. Have I been paying that the whole time?"

@Ruth"Do you pay the whole new balance by the due date?"

@Terrence"Every month."

@Ruth"Then you've never paid a cent of interest. That's the grace period. Pay the full statement balance by the due date, and your purchases cost nothing extra. Miss once, and the interest starts."

Terrence looked at the box at the bottom of the page. @Terrence"Then who is this warning for?"

@Ruth"It's for people who only pay the $35. The law makes the bank print it on the statement. The minimum keeps you out of trouble with the bank. It doesn't keep you out of interest."

@Terrence"Here's the part I don't get. I paid this card to zero on August tenth. Two days later, all three reports said I owed $340."

Ruth's chair creaked on the other end of the line. @Ruth"The card company reports your balance once a month. It sends the number from the end of your billing cycle. That's your closing date. By the time you paid, the number was already sent."

Terrence found the closing date on the statement. July eighteenth.

@Terrence"So if I pay before the eighteenth, the bureaus see a smaller number."

@Ruth"Same money. Different day."

Terrence almost laughed. He had fixed whole boilers with less.

@Terrence"One more thing. I was going to cancel the Summit card. I never use it, and the rate is 31.99%."

@Ruth"Don't. Right now your two limits add up to $1,700. Cancel the Summit card, and every bureau sees $1,200. That's the same thing TransUnion already got wrong. You'd just be doing it on purpose."

@Terrence"So I keep a card I don't use."

@Ruth"You keep a card you don't use, and you pay the other one in full. That's the whole trick."

After the call, Terrence sat with the statement for a long time. The Visa charged 23.99%, and he had never paid a dollar of it. The store card charged 31.99%, and he had never paid a dollar of that either.

The banks were not running these cards for free. Somebody was paying for them.

He folded the statement and put it back in his pocket. @Terrence"If it isn't me, then who is paying for my cards?"
