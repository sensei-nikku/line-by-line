## Track 0: Paid in Full

On Monday night, Terrence went back to the library with his notebook, the three reports, and the letter from Anchor Recovery.

Over the weekend, he had split his notebook into two lists. The first list, True, held the Desert Sun repossession and the Rapid Cash inquiry. Those lines hurt, but they were his. The second list was called Wrong, and he had written it out bureau by bureau.

On Experian, Meridian's $310 was under $500, so it should not have been there at all. Phoenix General and Sierra Asset were the same $840 bill, listed twice.

On Equifax, Anchor Recovery's $1,900 still showed up, even though it was paid. Meridian's $310 was there too, along with an address in Fresno, California, where he had never lived.

On TransUnion, it was Anchor Recovery's $1,900 again, and Meridian's $310.

One by one, he opened each bureau's website and found the page for disputes. For every line on his Wrong list, the site asked him to explain, so he kept each answer short and plain. This account is paid in full. This debt is listed twice. I have never lived at this address. For Anchor Recovery, he took a photo of the paid-in-full letter and uploaded it.

When he finished, each site gave him a confirmation number and the same message. The screen said: Most disputes are completed within 30 days. He copied all three numbers into his notebook and headed home.

At the kitchen table, he opened the file box again and pulled out last month's Copper State Visa statement. He had already paid it, and across the top, in pencil, he had written: Paid in full, August tenth.

This time, he went down the statement one line at a time. The closing date was July eighteenth, and the new balance was $340. The minimum payment was $35, due by August twelfth. His credit limit was $1,200, and the purchase APR was 23.99%.

Near the bottom was a box with a bold heading. The box said: Minimum Payment Warning. If you make only the minimum payment each period, you will pay more in interest and it will take you longer to pay off your balance.

Something didn't add up. He had paid this card down to zero, and yet all three reports, pulled on August twelfth, said he owed $340.

On Tuesday, he spent his lunch break in the boiler room with the statement on his knee. He called Valley Credit Union and asked for Ruth Okafor, the loan officer who had helped him the week before.

Ruth picked up on the second ring. @Ruth"This is Ruth."

@Terrence"Ruth, it's Terrence Whitfield. I filed those disputes last night, with all three bureaus."

@Ruth"Good. Now you wait about thirty days, and you don't apply for that car loan until you see what comes back."

@Terrence"I can wait. But I've got my card statement in my hand, and some of it doesn't make sense to me."

@Ruth"Then start at the top."

Terrence ran his thumb down to the rate. @Terrence"It says my APR is 23.99%. I've had this card eight years. Have I been paying that the whole time?"

@Ruth"Do you pay the whole new balance by the due date?"

@Terrence"Every month."

@Ruth"Then you've never paid a cent of interest. That's the grace period. When you pay the full statement balance by the due date, your purchases cost nothing extra. Miss it once, and the interest starts."

His eyes went to the box at the bottom of the page. @Terrence"Then who is this warning for?"

@Ruth"It's for people who only pay the $35. The law makes the bank print it on every statement. The minimum keeps you in good standing with the bank, but it doesn't keep you out of interest."

@Terrence"Here's the part I really don't get. I paid this card to zero on August tenth, and two days later, all three reports said I owed $340."

Ruth's chair creaked on the other end of the line. @Ruth"Your card company reports your balance once a month, and it sends the number from the end of your billing cycle. That's your closing date. By the time you paid, that number had already gone out."

Terrence found the closing date on the statement: July eighteenth. @Terrence"So if I pay before the eighteenth, the bureaus see a smaller number."

@Ruth"Same money. Different day."

He almost laughed, because he had fixed whole boilers with less to go on than that.

@Terrence"One more thing. I was going to cancel the Summit card. I never use it, and the rate on it is 31.99%."

@Ruth"Don't. Right now your two limits add up to $1,700. Cancel the Summit card, and every bureau sees $1,200 instead. That's the same thing TransUnion already got wrong, except you'd be doing it on purpose."

@Terrence"So I keep a card I don't even use."

@Ruth"You keep the card you don't use, and you pay the other one in full. That's the whole trick."

After the call, Terrence sat with the statement for a while. The Visa charged 23.99%, and he had never paid a dollar of it. The store card charged 31.99%, and he had never paid a dollar of that, either. Banks didn't run cards like these for free, so somebody was paying for them.

He folded the statement and slid it back into his pocket. @Terrence"If it isn't me, then who's paying for my cards?"
