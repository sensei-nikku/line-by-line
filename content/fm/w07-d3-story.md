## Track 0: The Way Out

On Thursday, Terrence signed for the small loan at Valley Credit Union: $500 at 18%, paid back at $87.76 a month for six months. On Saturday, he bought the Corolla. He put $1,260 down, and Valley lent him the other $5,040 for 48 months at 15.49%. His car payment was $141.52.

On Monday, he drove himself to work in the dark. He didn't open the ride app once, and he didn't miss it.

The next week, three envelopes came, one from each credit bureau. Terrence opened them at the kitchen table with his notebook beside him and checked each result against his Wrong list.

Equifax had removed the Fresno address and the paid Anchor Recovery bill. Experian had removed Meridian's $310 and the duplicate Phoenix General listing. TransUnion had removed Anchor Recovery and Meridian. Every line on his Wrong list was gone. The Copper State app showed a new score, 641, and Experian showed 628.

He called Ruth Okafor at the credit union.

[[Ruth said, ]]@Ruth"Good. Make six on-time car payments, then come see me about that rate."

@Terrence"The wrong lines are gone. But the debts behind some of them aren't."

[[Ruth said, ]]@Ruth"That's right. Taking a line off a report doesn't pay the bill. Bring me your list."

That night, he made one. Sierra Asset still held the $840 hospital bill, and it was his to pay. The collector had agreed to $35 a month. Meridian's $310 was off his reports, but he still owed it, and the hospital had set up a plan for $25 a month. He wrote all four debts in a table.

[[*Car loan: $5,040, 15.49%, $141.52 a month. Valley small loan: $500, 18%, $87.76 a month. Sierra Asset collection: $840, no interest, $35 a month. Meridian medical bill: $310, no interest, $25 a month.*]]{{The car loan was $5,040 at 15.49%, with a payment of $141.52. The small loan was $500 at 18%, at $87.76 a month. Sierra Asset was $840 at no interest, at $35 a month. And Meridian was $310 at no interest, at $25 a month.}}

The minimums came to $289.28 a month. He made $3,500 a month before taxes. Without the rides, he could pay $150 more than the minimums every month.

One old line wasn't on the list. The Desert Sun repossession still said he owed $8,412. When he asked about it, Ruth didn't hesitate.

@Ruth"That one's different. Don't pay anything on it until you talk to legal aid. Ask them when the clock started."

On Saturday morning, he sat across from Ruth's desk with the list. She had a fresh cup of coffee she wasn't drinking.

@Ruth"Pay every minimum, every month. Then the extra $150 goes to one debt at a time. There are two ways to pick which one."

She turned his list toward him and tapped the rate column. @Ruth"The avalanche goes to the highest rate first. That saves the most money."

Then she tapped the balance column. @Ruth"The snowball goes to the smallest balance first. You finish a whole debt fast, and that keeps a lot of people going."

Terrence ran his pencil down the rates. @Terrence"So with the avalanche, my two collections come last. They don't charge interest."

[[Ruth said, ]]@Ruth"They don't. But they're still on your report until they're paid. Some scoring formulas stop counting a collection once it's paid. Some don't."

@Terrence"And with the snowball, the collections go first, and the small loan keeps charging me 18% while I pay them."

[[Ruth said, ]]@Ruth"Both plans work if you stick with them. Run both, and pick the one you'll finish."

That night, Terrence drew two columns in his notebook and wrote Avalanche over one and Snowball over the other. Under each, he listed the four debts in a different order.

He looked at the two columns for a long time. @Terrence"One way saves me money, and one way clears my name faster. So which one is my way out?"
