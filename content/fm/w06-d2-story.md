## Track 0: Two Numbers

The next day, Terrence ate lunch in the boiler room of the elementary school where he was working that week. He opened the Copper State Visa app to check his balance. Under the balance was a button he had never tapped. It said: See your free credit score.

He tapped it.

The screen said: Your FICO Score is 604. Based on your TransUnion report. Scores range from 300 to 850.

Terrence looked at the number for a long time. It did not sound good. It did not sound terrible. He did not know what it sounded like.

That evening, back at the library, he made a free account on Experian's website. It showed him a score too. The screen said: Your FICO Score is 581. Based on your Experian report.

Terrence opened his notebook to a clean page. At the top he wrote both numbers. 604. 581. Same man. Same day. Twenty-three points apart.

On Saturday morning, he went back to Valley Credit Union. Ruth Okafor was at her desk. She had her reading glasses on the chain and a full cup of coffee she was not drinking.

@Terrence"I've got two credit scores," Terrence said. @Terrence"Which one is the real one?"

@Ruth"Both of them," Ruth said. @Ruth"And neither one is the only one."

She turned her chair to face him.

@Ruth"A credit score is a formula," Ruth said. @Ruth"It reads one report and gives back one number. A different report gives a different number. A different formula gives a different number. You don't have one credit score. You have a lot of them."

@Terrence"So which one does a lender look at?" Terrence asked.

@Ruth"Whichever one that lender picks," Ruth said. @Ruth"Some pull one bureau. Some pull all three. That's why you fix all three reports, not just one."

She pulled a deposit slip out of the cup by her keyboard and turned it over. She wrote five lines on the back and slid it across the desk.

The slip said: Payment history, 35%. Amounts owed, 30%. Length of credit history, 15%. New credit, 10%. Credit mix, 10%.

@Ruth"That's what goes into a FICO score," Ruth said. @Ruth"Biggest one first."

She tapped the first line with her pen.

@Ruth"Payment history," Ruth said. @Ruth"Did you pay on time? Collections count here too. That car repossession sits in this line. It's true, so it stays until 2029."

@Terrence"That one's mine," Terrence said. @Terrence"I know."

She tapped the second line.

@Ruth"Amounts owed," Ruth said. @Ruth"How much you owe, and how much of your card limits you're using."

Terrence opened his notebook to the page with the three columns.

@Terrence"TransUnion doesn't have my Summit card," Terrence said. @Terrence"So it thinks I'm using more of my limit than I am."

@Ruth"Then that's one reason your two numbers don't match," Ruth said. @Ruth"The formula can only read what's on the report. It doesn't know what's missing."

Terrence ran his finger down the Experian column. It said: Phoenix General, $840. A few lines down, it said: Sierra Asset, $840.

@Terrence"What about this?" Terrence asked. @Terrence"Same bill, two names."

@Ruth"Then it looks like you owe that bill twice," Ruth said. @Ruth"That's not a small thing."

She tapped the third line.

@Ruth"Length of credit history," Ruth said. @Ruth"Your oldest account is that Valley loan. You opened it in 2015. It's paid off, and it's still helping you. Leave it alone."

Terrence almost smiled. He had been proud of that zero.

She tapped the fourth line.

@Ruth"New credit," Ruth said. @Ruth"Every time you apply for something, the lender pulls your file. That's a hard inquiry. Each one counts a little, for a while."

Terrence counted them in his notebook. Experian showed two. Equifax showed three. TransUnion showed one. The extra one on Equifax said: Rapid Cash of Arizona, September 2025.

He remembered that one. Last September, the car needed brakes, and payday was nine days away. He had filled out the form at Rapid Cash. He had read the fee at the bottom of the page. Then he had walked out without signing. They had pulled his credit anyway.

@Terrence"That one's real," Terrence said. @Terrence"I applied. I just didn't take the money."

@Ruth"Then it isn't an error," Ruth said. @Ruth"It comes off in two years."

Terrence frowned at the notebook.

@Terrence"If I apply for a car loan here and at two other places," Terrence said, @Terrence"that's three more of these."

@Ruth"Not if you shop fast," Ruth said. @Ruth"When you shop for the same kind of loan, like a car loan, the formulas count inquiries that are close together as one. Do all your shopping inside fourteen days. Credit cards don't get that deal. Every card application counts."

She tapped the last line.

@Ruth"Credit mix," Ruth said. @Ruth"Cards, loans, a mortgage. Don't go borrowing money just to fix that one."

Terrence picked up the slip and read it again.

@Terrence"There's nothing on here about my paycheck," Terrence said. @Terrence"Or my savings."

@Ruth"That's right," Ruth said. @Ruth"Your report doesn't list your income or your savings. So your score can't see them. It only sees your history with borrowed money."

On the bus home, Terrence held the deposit slip in one hand and the notebook in the other. Some lines on his reports were true, and they hurt. Some lines were wrong, and they hurt. Some lines were different from bureau to bureau, but they were not wrong at all.

He looked at the two numbers at the top of the page. 604. 581.

@Terrence"Which of these lines are wrong," Terrence said quietly, @Terrence"and what are they costing me?"
